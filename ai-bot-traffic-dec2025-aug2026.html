The key insight

9 in 10

visits to our website in August came from bots, not people: 3.8 million bot requests against 366,000 human visits. In December, bots were already 83% — in June they reached 96%.

Share of all traffic per month · bot requests vs human visits

People have become a small slice of who visits our website

A human visit (a “session”) usually loads several pages; a bot request is one file. Counted against human page views instead, bots were still 77% of traffic in August (55% in December). No bot export exists for January 2026.

Real visitors · sessions per day

The June dip was a two-week drop — and the bots didn’t notice it

From 15 to 29 June, visits fell from about 12,500 to about 2,800 a day. Bots kept coming at roughly 170,000 requests a day, so the site was online. This looks more like a gap in Google Analytics measurement than people staying away — worth checking with the web team.

Bytespider · ByteDance

Bytespider went from zero to 6 million requests a month in just three months

It first appeared in February. Since May it has dropped by two thirds — but it is still our single biggest visitor, at 52% of all bot traffic in August.

GPTBot · OpenAI

GPTBot went quiet for three months — then came back busier than ever

From mid-April to mid-July it made almost no requests. From 19 July it ran at up to 70,000 a day, making August its biggest month: 1.3 million requests. In the last week of August it slowed down again.

Share of top-20 requests that were code or style files

Asking for pages that don’t exist

Since February, Bytespider has topped the “page not found” list every month, repeatedly asking for files we never had — like /files/sdk.min.js and /undefined.

In August, visitors calling themselves Googlebot tried WordPress login pages (wp-login.php) — a pattern more typical of an impostor than of Google. Worth a check by IT.

What this means

Six things to take away

Bots outnumber people

In August, 91% of traffic was bots: about 10 bot requests for every human visit, up from 5 in December.

People: down, then recovering

Human visits peaked in January (634,000), fell to 227,000 in June and were back to 366,000 in August.

Check the June numbers

Visits collapsed for two weeks while bots carried on as usual — likely a measurement gap, not a real loss.

AI now outweighs search

In December, Google Search was most bot traffic. By August, nearly 9 in 10 bot requests came from AI companies.

One bot drives the volume

Bytespider caused the May peak and is still half of all bot traffic, often asking for code files and missing pages.

OpenAI is back, and bigger

GPTBot returned in late July and was the biggest bot by data volume in August. Bots take 74% more data than in December.

For discussion: Do we want to limit Bytespider? Do we want our content in AI answers (ChatGPT, Perplexity)? Should IT look into the August login-page probes? Can the web team confirm what happened to Google Analytics in late June?

About the data

Where these numbers come from

Bot data
Monthly exports of the “AI traffic – Corporate application” dashboard: requests, data volume, response codes and addresses per bot. No export for January 2026.
Human data
Google Analytics (GA4) for maastrichtuniversity.nl: daily visits (sessions), users and page views, December 2025 – August 2026, January included.
Visits vs requests
“Bot share” compares bot requests with human sessions. A session usually contains several page views, so the share is also shown against page views.
Human visits are a floor
Google Analytics can miss people who block tracking or decline cookies, so the real number of people is likely somewhat higher.
June 15–29
Human visits dropped sharply while bot traffic stayed normal. Treat June’s human total with caution until the cause is known.
Month boundaries
Bot exports run from the 1st of the month to the 1st of the next, starting at slightly different times of day. Small overlaps don’t change the trends.
Top addresses
The bot dashboard lists only the 20 most-requested addresses per month; the code-file share is based on those.
Bot names
Bots identify themselves by name. A few may be impostors using a well-known name.
